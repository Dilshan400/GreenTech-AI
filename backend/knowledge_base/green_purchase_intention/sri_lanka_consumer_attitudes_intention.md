# The Impact of Consumers' Attitudes on Green Purchase Intention in Southern Sri Lanka

**Authors**: R. M. P. D. Rathnayaka & T. S. L. W. Gunawardana (Faculty of Management and Finance, University of Ruhuna, Sri Lanka)  
**Publication**: *Wayamba Journal of Management*, Vol. 12, Issue 1, pp. 146–170.

---

### Research Context & Objectives
Conducted in the Galle District of Sri Lanka, this empirical investigation evaluates the impact of consumer attitudes on green purchasing intentions, aiming to clarify conflicting findings in literature regarding antecedent vs. mediating psychological constructs.

The study investigates four dimensions of consumer attitudes:
1. **Social Influence**: The extent to which interpersonal networks (friends, family, colleagues) persuade or normalize eco-friendly purchases.
2. **Environmental Consciousness**: Factual awareness and emotional involvement regarding environmental degradation and conservation.
3. **Environmental Responsibility**: The personal moral obligation felt by individuals to actively protect the environment.
4. **Health Consciousness**: The desire to select healthy, chemical-free, or non-hazardous products to safeguard personal and public well-being.

---

### Methodology
* **Sample & Setting**: Structured questionnaires administered to green product consumers across the Galle District ($51.6\%$ male, $48.4\%$ female; $65\%$ under 40 years old; $78.5\%$ executive-level employees).
* **Analytical Framework**: Evaluated using **Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM)** with SmartPLS, establishing construct reliability, convergent validity (AVE $> 0.50$, Composite Reliability $> 0.70$), and discriminant validity via Fornell-Larcker criteria.

---

### Key Statistical Results
Evaluating the structural path model with bootstrapping ($N = 93$ validated green consumers):

| Structural Path | Path Coefficient ($\beta$) | $t$-statistic | $p$-value | Empirical Conclusion |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Social Influence $\rightarrow$ Green Purchase Intention** | **$0.393$** | **$4.215$** | **$p < 0.001$** | **Supported (Significant)** |
| **Environmental Consciousness $\rightarrow$ Green Purchase Intention** | **$0.333$** | **$3.396$** | **$p < 0.001$** | **Supported (Significant)** |
| **Environmental Responsibility $\rightarrow$ Green Purchase Intention** | $0.178$ | $1.659$ | $p > 0.05$ | Not Supported (Insignificant) |
| **Health Consciousness $\rightarrow$ Green Purchase Intention** | $0.030$ | $0.292$ | $p > 0.05$ | Not Supported (Insignificant) |

* **Overall Model Fit**: Standardized Root Mean Square Residual ($\text{SRMR} = 0.098$), Normed Fit Index ($\text{NFI} = 0.535$).

---

### Core Theoretical & Practical Insights
1. **Peers and Social Networks are the Strongest Driver**: Social influence ($\beta = 0.393$) emerged as the most powerful determinant of green purchase intention. When peers and social groups actively discuss eco-products and share environmental info, consumer buying intentions surge.
2. **Consciousness Over Abstract Responsibility**: While generalized environmental consciousness ($\beta = 0.333$) directly stimulates buying intent, personal feelings of abstract "environmental responsibility" failed to reach statistical significance. Consumers often believe government or large corporations bear primary environmental responsibility rather than individuals.
3. **Strategic Recommendation for Green Tech Marketing**:
   - Focus promotional campaigns on peer endorsements, community group discussions, and social media sharing.
   - Emphasize direct, tangible facts about product eco-benefits rather than guilt-based responsibility appeals.
