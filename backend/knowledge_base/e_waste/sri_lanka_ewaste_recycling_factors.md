# Factors Affecting E-Waste Recycling and eCycling Intention in Sri Lanka

**Authors**: K. S. H. Sarathchandra & H. A. H. Hettiarachchi (Department of Commerce & Financial Management, University of Kelaniya, Sri Lanka)  
**Publication**: *International Journal of Scientific and Research Publications (IJSRP)*, Volume 10, Issue 1, January 2020, pp. 101–107.

---

### Research Background & Sri Lankan E-Waste Context
With the rapid proliferation of electrical and electronic equipment (EEE)—such as personal computers, mobile phones, printers, and home appliances—e-waste accumulation has accelerated dramatically across Sri Lanka.
* According to Sri Lanka Customs data cited in the study:
  - Mobile phone annual imports: **1,200,000 units** (average lifespan: 3 years; forecasted annual growth: 40%).
  - PC annual imports: **300,000 units** (average lifespan: 6 years).
  - Batteries: **6,000,000 units** imported annually.
* Discarded e-waste contains hazardous heavy metals including lead ($\text{Pb}$), cadmium ($\text{Cd}$), mercury ($\text{Hg}$), barium, and brominated flame retardants. In developing countries like Sri Lanka, inadequate municipal e-waste recycling infrastructure poses acute environmental and public health hazards (toxic leaching into groundwater and agricultural soil).

---

### Theoretical Model & Hypotheses
The study employs the **Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA)** and the **Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)** to investigate five predictor variables on **eCycling Behavioral Intention (Willingness)**:
1. **Attitude toward eCycling**: Individual beliefs and evaluations regarding the necessity and benefits of eCycling.
2. **Perceived Knowledge**: Level of factual understanding regarding hazardous e-waste handling.
3. **Awareness of Consequences**: Consciousness of environmental hazards caused by improper disposal.
4. **Subjective Norms**: Social pressure from peers, family, and university communities.
5. **Perceived Convenience**: Time, physical proximity, and perceived effort needed to drop off or recycle obsolete electronics.

---

### Empirical Findings
Conducted via a structured questionnaire among **245 university students and households** at the University of Kelaniya, Sri Lanka:

1. **Correlation Analysis**:
   - All five constructs demonstrated statistically significant positive correlations with eCycling willingness ($p < 0.05$).
   - **Perceived Convenience** exhibited the highest correlation with willingness ($r = 0.414$), followed by **Perceived Knowledge** ($r = 0.302$), Awareness of Consequences ($r = 0.288$), Attitude ($r = 0.268$), and Subjective Norms ($r = 0.260$).
2. **Multiple Regression Results ($R^2 = 0.240, F = 14.975, p < 0.001$)**:
   - **Perceived Convenience** was the strongest and most significant predictor ($\beta = 0.299, p = 0.000$).
   - **Attitude toward eCycling** was also a statistically significant positive predictor ($\beta = 0.166, p = 0.020$).
   - Knowledge ($\beta = 0.006, p = 0.942$), Awareness of Consequences ($\beta = 0.125, p = 0.083$), and Subjective Norms ($\beta = 0.125, p = 0.100$) were not statistically significant in the final regression model.

---

### Key Takeaways for IT Students & E-Waste Infrastructure
* **Convenience is Paramount**: Even environmentally conscious consumers and students will not recycle their electronic equipment if collection centers are distant, hard to locate, or require cumbersome drop-off procedures.
* **Campus & Municipal Recommendations**:
  - Educational institutions and municipal authorities should set up accessible e-waste collection bins directly inside university campuses, shopping centers, and telecommunication service centers.
  - Implement periodic university e-waste collection drives in coordination with licensed Central Environmental Authority (CEA) recyclers to lower the barrier of perceived inconvenience.
